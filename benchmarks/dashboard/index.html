      Direct cable · Mac ↔ mars

      What 10 GbE is actually useful for

      These results come from a direct 10 GbE cable between the MacBook Pro (M1 Max, OWC Thunderbolt 10G adapter) and mars (Ryzen 9 7900X, Intel X540), which carries 9.41 Gb/s of raw TCP each way. Only runs made once the link ran at full speed are counted; earlier runs on the same cable topped out near 3 Gb/s and are left out. Rates are the data delivered: the size of what was copied over the time to have it safely on disk.

      In short: 10 GbE pays off when most of the bytes are in files of a megabyte or more and the tool can keep several streams busy. When files average a few kilobytes, the receiving disk creating files sets the pace, well under 1 Gb/s, and gigabit is already enough.

      Large files: this is what it is for

      Files of hundreds of megabytes and up move at 8.5 to 9 Gb/s with xs from mars to the Mac: about nine times what gigabit carries, and close to the 9.41 Gb/s raw ceiling. xs splits files into blocks and keeps several workers sending, so one copy can fill the link. rsync stops at 2.9 to 3.3 Gb/s whatever the link: one thread on each side does all the work. With rsync, 10 GbE buys about three times gigabit, not ten.

      Mixed trees: it depends on where the bytes are

      A tree of 1,500 files averaging about a megabyte still runs at 6 to 7.4 Gb/s. A tree of 20,000 files, mostly small with a few large ones, reaches about 7 Gb/s onto mars's NVMe because most of its bytes are in the large files, but only 1.9 to 2.6 Gb/s onto the Mac, where APFS creating the small files holds it back.

      Small files: gigabit is enough

      100,000 files of about 3 KB copy at 0.05 to 0.7 Gb/s on this link, under what gigabit carries. The limit is the receiver creating files: up to about 28,000 a second onto mars (fewer in a deep tree), 1,500 to 7,000 onto the Mac. The 216,000-file congress data set (5 KB a file) runs at 0.1 to 0.6 Gb/s. A faster network changes nothing here; fewer, larger files would (an archive, or a database instead of a directory of records), or a filesystem that creates files faster.

      Syncs where little changed: the link hardly matters

      Re-running a sync over an unchanged tree sends file lists, not file contents, so the time goes to walking and comparing directories. xs checks 100,000 unchanged files in a flat tree about three times faster than rsync, but on gigabit it would take little longer: the network is not what it waits for.

      Compression: only with a fast codec

      On text that compresses about four times, xs at zstd level 1 delivered more than 9 Gb/s of data from mars to the Mac, more than the same files without compression in the same session. Level 3 cannot compress fast enough to keep 10 GbE full on these machines, and rsync's single-threaded -z stays between 0.7 and 2 Gb/s. On data that is half incompressible the gain is smaller, from a few percent to half again, and which level wins varies. Whether zstd's faster levels or LZ4 do better is still being measured.

      Caveats

      The Mac was heavily loaded during the tuning and compression runs (load averages of 75 to 250), so the Mac's side of those runs is slower than it would be on a quiet machine, and copies from the Mac vary more than copies to it. Some of these runs are still in progress; the charts and the table update as samples arrive, the text was written on 16 October 2026.

        The pitch

        A terabyte before the coffee goes cold

        Disclosure: what follows was written by Claude, an AI, imitating the style of Hunter S. Thompson. It is a pastiche, not his words, and he had nothing to do with it. The numbers in it are the measured ones from this page.

        It was past two in the morning when the numbers came in, and they were savage. Eight and a half, nine gigabits a second, a fat single-file firehose screaming down one copper cable from the big AMD box to the laptop, and the laptop taking it, gulping it, every byte landing on disk and synced like a signed confession. Nine times what the gigabit wire could ever carry. I had seen rsync choke at three on the same cable, one thread grinding like a man trying to bail out a lake with a coffee cup, and I understood that the wire had never been the problem. The wire was waiting for us.

        Consider the terabyte. Every serious person has one now: four hours of camera footage, a season of raw photographs, the virtual machine you swore you would delete, the model weights some committee decided you needed. On gigabit that terabyte crawls across in about two and a half hours, long enough to lose faith in the whole enterprise. On this link, with a tool that keeps its workers moving, it is fifteen minutes. Fifteen. You start the copy, you make the coffee, and the thing is done before the cup is cool enough to drink. That is not a benchmark. That is a different way to live.

        But hear me, because the swine who sell switches will not tell you this: the fast wire is useless for a hundred thousand tiny files. I watched them go at a fraction of a gigabit, the receiving disk creating them one at a time like a clerk stamping forms in triplicate, and no amount of copper would have hurried it. Ten gig is for the big stuff: footage, disk images, backups, datasets, the heavy freight. Put your small files in an archive, buy the card, run the cable, and stop waiting on your own machines. The future arrives at nine gigabits a second, and it does not care whether you are ready.
